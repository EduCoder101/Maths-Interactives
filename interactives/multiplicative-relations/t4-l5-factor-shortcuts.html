Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to split a number into factors so we can multiply and divide in our heads.

Success Criteria

  • I can split a number into a factor pair.
  • I can multiply by a 2-digit number by multiplying by its factors one at a time.
  • I can divide by 4 or 8 by halving two or three times.
  • I can divide by a number like 6 or 12 by dividing by its factors one at a time, then check my answer.

The big idea

Big numbers are built from factors. Split one into its factors and take one easy step at a time.

To multiply

28 = 4 × 7

So multiplying by 28 is the same as multiplying by 4, then by 7.

To divide

8 = 2 × 2 × 2

So dividing by 8 is the same as halving, halving and halving again.

Card 7 of 13

A common mistake: splitting the divisor by adding

A student worked out 120 ÷ 12 by splitting 12 into 10 + 2. Is she right?

The student's working
1.12 = 10 + 2
2.120 ÷ 10 = 12
3.120 ÷ 2 = 60
4.12 + 60 = 72
But 120 ÷ 12 = 10, not 72.
72 lots of 12 is far more than 120.
Split by multiplying
1.12 = 2 × 6
2.120 ÷ 2 = 60
3.60 ÷ 6 = 10
Check: 10 × 12 = 120. Correct.

Split the divisor by multiplying, never by adding.

In your book

Write the rule: Split the divisor by multiplying, never by adding. Then copy the green working.

Remember Term 1, Lesson 2: prime and composite

6, 12 and 15 are composite: they have more than two factors. 7, 11 and 13 are prime: their only factors are 1 and the number itself.

So what do we do?

Composite divisor: split it and use the factor shortcut. Prime divisor: this shortcut does not work, so use another strategy, like chunking with the area model.

Question 1

Is 1,260 ÷ 36 easier as ÷ 4 then ÷ 9, or ÷ 6 then ÷ 6? Try both and explain.

Route A: 36 = 4 × 9
1,260 ÷ 2 = 630
630 ÷ 2 = 315
315 ÷ 9 = 35
Route B: 36 = 6 × 6
1,260 ÷ 6 = 210
210 ÷ 6 = 35
Check: 35 × 36 = 1,260
Both routes give 35. Route B has fewer steps, and 1,260 ÷ 6 is friendly because 12 ÷ 6 = 2. Route A uses easy halving, but 315 ÷ 9 is harder. Either is fine if you explain your choice.
Question 2

Can you divide by 7 using factors? Why not?

No. 7 is a prime number. Its only factors are 1 and 7, so the only split is 1 × 7. Dividing by 1 does not change anything, so the shortcut cannot help. Use a different strategy, like chunking.

Go Deeper: Factor Investigations

These tasks will push your thinking beyond finding answers. You will explore, make conjectures, and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
Work out 720 ÷ 24 in three different ways by splitting 24 into different factor pairs, for example 4 × 6, 3 × 8 and 2 × 12.
What do you notice about your three answers? Why do you think that happens?
A mathematician's response

Before I started, I made an estimate. 25 × 30 = 750, so 720 ÷ 24 should be close to 30.

My first idea was to split 24 into 20 + 4. I worked out 720 ÷ 20 = 36 and 720 ÷ 4 = 180, then added them to get 216. That is nowhere near 30! Then I remembered that 20 + 4 is adding, and we split the divisor by multiplying. So I started again.

Way 1: 24 = 4 × 6
720 ÷ 4 = 180 (I halved twice)
180 ÷ 6 = 30

Way 2: 24 = 3 × 8
720 ÷ 3 = 240
240 ÷ 8 = 30 (I halved three times)

Way 3: 24 = 2 × 12
720 ÷ 2 = 360
360 ÷ 12 = 30. This was the hardest one for me, because dividing by 12 is still a big step.

All three ways gave 30, which matches my estimate. It does not seem to matter which factor pair I use, as long as the two factors multiply to make 24. Some pairs are just easier to work with than others.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
A student says: "Dividing by 6 and then dividing by 2 gives the same answer as dividing by 8." Is this always true, sometimes true or never true, for any whole number bigger than 0?
Test it with at least five different numbers. Then write your conjecture and explain why you think it happens.
A mathematician's response

At first I thought it might be true, because 6 + 2 = 8. So I tried 48. 48 ÷ 6 = 8, then 8 ÷ 2 = 4. But 48 ÷ 8 = 6. Those are different! I wondered if I had made a slip, so I tested more numbers.

Every time, the "÷ 6 then ÷ 2" answer was smaller than the "÷ 8" answer. Then I noticed those smaller answers matched dividing by 12: 96 ÷ 12 = 8 and 240 ÷ 12 = 20.

That makes sense, because 6 × 2 = 12. Dividing by 6 and then by 2 is the same as dividing by 12, not 8. The idea that 6 + 2 = 8 was the adding mistake again.

My conjecture: never true. Dividing by 12 always gives a smaller answer than dividing by 8, for any whole number bigger than 0. (With 0 both answers would be 0, which is why the question says bigger than 0.)

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be proved.
Generalising
Which divisors from 2 to 20 can be split into two smaller factors (neither of them 1) so you can use the split-the-divisor shortcut? Write a rule that tells you whether any divisor can be split.
Sort every whole number from 2 to 20 into two groups. What do the numbers that cannot be split have in common?
A mathematician's response

I checked each number one at a time. 4 = 2 × 2, 6 = 2 × 3, 8 = 2 × 4, 9 = 3 × 3, 10 = 2 × 5, and so on. Then I sorted them.

Can be split: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20
Cannot be split: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19

At first I thought the rule was "even numbers can be split and odd numbers cannot". Then 2 broke my rule: it is even, but its only factors are 1 and 2. And 9 and 15 broke it too: they are odd, but 9 = 3 × 3 and 15 = 3 × 5. So odd and even was the wrong idea.

I looked at the "cannot" group again: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19. These are the prime numbers from Term 1! A prime number has exactly two factors, 1 and itself, so there is no way to split it without using 1.

My rule: a composite divisor can be split into smaller factors, so the shortcut works. A prime divisor cannot be split, so I need a different strategy.

Mathematician's thinking: You are generalising. That means you are making a rule that works every time, not just for the examples you tried.
