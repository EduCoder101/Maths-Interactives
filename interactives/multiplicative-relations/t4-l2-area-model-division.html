Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to use the area model to divide by chipping away friendly multiples of the divisor.

Success Criteria

  • I can draw an area model with the divisor as the side I know.
  • I can chip away a friendly multiple of the divisor, such as 10 or 100 groups.
  • I can keep chipping until what is left is smaller than the divisor, and record it as the remainder.
  • I can add the section widths to find the quotient and check that divisor × quotient + remainder = dividend.

The Big Idea

In Term 1 we used the area model in reverse to find a missing side.

Today we use it to chip away at any division, even when something is left over.

Term 1: the area model in reverse

We knew the area and one side. Every division came out exactly, with nothing left over.

Today: chip away

We chip off friendly chunks, one at a time. If a little bit is left that is too small for another group, that is the remainder.

Key Idea: When Do We Stop?

When what is left is smaller than the divisor, stop.
That leftover is the remainder.

divisor × quotient + remainder = dividend

Why stop?

If what is left were as big as the divisor, one more whole group would fit. So we would keep chipping.

Why check?

Multiplying the quotient by the divisor and adding the remainder must take us all the way back to the number we started with.

In your book

Copy both key ideas in a box. Learn the check equation by heart.

🔓 Go Deeper: Remainder Investigations

These tasks will push your thinking beyond finding answers. You will explore, make conjectures, and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
Divide each of these numbers by 4: 50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57. Record the remainder each time.
What do you notice about the list of remainders?
A mathematician's response

I started with 50. I chipped away 4 × 10 = 40, which left 10. Then 4 × 2 = 8, which left 2. So 50 ÷ 4 = 12 remainder 2.

51 is one more, so the leftover grew by one: 12 remainder 3. I guessed that 52 would be 12 remainder 4. But that was wrong. A leftover of 4 is a whole group of 4, so one more group fits: 52 ÷ 4 = 13 remainder 0.

After that I kept going: 53 gave remainder 1, 54 gave 2, 55 gave 3, 56 gave 0 and 57 gave 1.

My list of remainders is 2, 3, 0, 1, 2, 3, 0, 1. The remainders go round in a cycle of 0, 1, 2, 3. They never reached 4.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
Sam says: "When you divide a whole number by 6, the remainder can be 6."
Is Sam's statement always true, sometimes true or never true? Test some numbers, including small ones, and write your conjecture.
A mathematician's response

I tested some numbers. 0 ÷ 6 = 0 remainder 0. 5 ÷ 6 = 0 remainder 5, because not even one group of 6 fits into 5. 6 ÷ 6 = 1 remainder 0.

Then I tried 12. I chipped away one group of 6 and had 6 left. I thought, "There it is, remainder 6! Sam is sometimes right." But then I looked again. I had stopped too early. 6 is not smaller than 6, so another group fits. 12 ÷ 6 = 2 remainder 0.

I tried two more: 17 ÷ 6 = 2 remainder 5, and 18 ÷ 6 = 3 remainder 0. The biggest remainder I ever got was 5.

My conjecture: Sam's statement is never true. When you divide by 6, the remainder can only be 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or 5. A leftover of 6 is always one more group of 6.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be proved.
Justifying
A whole number is divided by 7. The quotient is 23. The remainder can be any whole number that is allowed, including 0. What is the smallest the number could be? What is the largest?
Find both numbers. Then explain, using the area model, why 168 is not possible.
A mathematician's response

I drew an area model with 7 as the side I know and 23 as the width. That makes 7 × 23 = 161. With a remainder of 0, the number is 161. That is the smallest.

For the largest, my first idea was to add a remainder of 7: 161 + 7 = 168. Then I remembered the rule from the lesson. The remainder must be smaller than the divisor. 7 is not smaller than 7, so that does not work.

The biggest remainder allowed is 6. So the largest number is 161 + 6 = 167. Check: 7 × 23 + 6 = 167.

Why is 168 not possible? In the area model, a leftover strip of 7 would be exactly as tall as the side of 7. That means it is a full column, one whole extra group of 7. So the box gets one wider: 7 × 24 = 168. The quotient of 168 ÷ 7 is 24 with remainder 0, not 23.

So the number can be anything from 161 to 167.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just finding an answer, you are explaining why it must be true, and why other answers cannot work.
