Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to use a calculator to divide by 2-digit numbers, make sense of the display, and check the answer by multiplying.

Success Criteria

  • I can estimate before I press any buttons.
  • I can read the whole-number part of a calculator answer as the number of full groups.
  • I can find the remainder by multiplying back and subtracting.
  • I can check a division by multiplying a 3-digit number by a 2-digit number.

The Big Idea

The digits after the decimal point are not the remainder.
Multiply back to find it.
Class Calculator
250÷12=
20.83333333

The whole-number part (yellow) tells you the full groups.

The decimal part is part of one more group. It is not a count of leftovers.

Card 3 of 12

The Four-Step Calculator Routine

1

Radar estimate

Round to friendly numbers and estimate before you press any buttons.

2

Calculate and read the whole-number part

The whole-number part of the display is the number of full groups.

3

Multiply back and subtract

Divisor × full groups, then dividend − that answer. What is left is the remainder.

4

Check by hand

divisor × quotient + remainder = dividend

In your book

Copy the four steps. You will use them for every question today.

Two Key Ideas

If the display shows a whole number, the remainder is 0.
Class Calculator
864÷27=
32
27 × 32 = 864

Nothing is left over. 27 goes into 864 exactly 32 times.

The remainder must always be smaller than the divisor.

Dividing by 23? The remainder can only be 0 up to 22.

If you get 23 or more left over, there is enough to make another full group. Go back and check your full groups.

🔓 Go Deeper: Calculator Investigations

These tasks will push your thinking beyond finding answers. You will explore, make conjectures, and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
77 ÷ 5 shows 15.4. Multiply the decimal part by the divisor: 0.4 × 5 = 2. Check: 5 × 15 + 2 = 77.
Now try 47 ÷ 4, 38 ÷ 5 and 63 ÷ 10. For each one, multiply the decimal part by the divisor, then check by multiplying back.
What do you notice each time?
A mathematician's response

I started with 47 ÷ 4. The calculator showed 11.75. My first thought was that the remainder was 75, because those are the digits after the point. But that can't be right: 75 is way bigger than 4, and a remainder has to be smaller than the divisor.

So I did what the task said. 0.75 is three quarters, and three quarters of 4 is 3. So 0.75 × 4 = 3. Check: 4 × 11 = 44, and 44 + 3 = 47. It works.

Next, 38 ÷ 5 showed 7.6. 0.6 × 5 = 3. Check: 5 × 7 = 35, and 35 + 3 = 38. Works again.

Then 63 ÷ 10 showed 6.3. 0.3 × 10 = 3. Check: 10 × 6 = 60, and 60 + 3 = 63.

What I notice: every time, the decimal part times the divisor gave me exactly the remainder. The decimal part seems to be "how much of one more group" is left. It is funny that all three remainders were 3, but the displays all looked different. So the decimal digits do not show the remainder directly, but they do hide it.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
Mia says: "The remainder is always the decimal part times the divisor."
Test Mia's method on 250 ÷ 12 (the display shows 20.83333333) and on 1,000 ÷ 24 (the display shows 41.66666667). Type the decimal part into your calculator and multiply by the divisor.
Is Mia's method always reliable on a calculator? Write your conjecture and the evidence for it.
A mathematician's response

I typed 0.83333333 × 12 and the calculator said 9.99999996. At first I thought Mia was just wrong, because a remainder has to be a whole number and 9.99999996 is not.

Then I multiplied back to find the real remainder: 12 × 20 = 240, and 250 − 240 = 10. So the real remainder is 10, and Mia's answer was super close to 10, but not exactly 10.

I tried the second one: 0.66666667 × 24 = 16.00000008. Multiplying back: 24 × 41 = 984, and 1,000 − 984 = 16. Close again, but this time a tiny bit too big.

Why? The decimal 0.8333... goes on forever, but the calculator only has room for 10 digits, so it cuts it off or rounds it. Multiplying a cut-off number gives a cut-off answer. With 15.4 in Task 1 the decimal stopped, so the answer came out exact.

My conjecture: Mia's idea is true, but on a calculator it is not always reliable. When the decimal goes on forever you get a number very close to the remainder, not the exact remainder. Multiplying back with whole numbers is safer because whole numbers never get rounded.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be proved.
Justifying
A whole number was divided by 8, and the calculator showed 26.375. What was the whole number, and what was the remainder? Could more than one whole number give exactly this display?
Find the number and the remainder. Then justify how many answers there are.
A mathematician's response

The whole-number part is 26, so there were 26 full groups of 8. 26 × 8 = 208. My first guess was that the number was 208. But 208 ÷ 8 = 26 exactly, and the display had .375 on the end. So something was left over that I had forgotten.

Using Task 1: decimal part times divisor. 0.375 × 8 = 3. So the remainder is 3, and the number is 208 + 3 = 211. I checked: 211 ÷ 8 = 26.375. Yes.

Could another number work? I tried the neighbours. 210 ÷ 8 = 26.25 and 212 ÷ 8 = 26.5. Each time I add 1 to the number, the display goes up by one eighth of a group, which is 0.125. So the displays go 26.25, 26.375, 26.5, and only 211 lands on 26.375.

So there is only one answer: 211, remainder 3. Every whole number gives its own different display when you divide by 8.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just finding an answer, you are explaining why it must be the only answer. A reason that works for every case is stronger than one example.
