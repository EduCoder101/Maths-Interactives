Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to choose and apply partitioning, levelling and constant difference to solve addition and subtraction problems efficiently.

Success Criteria

  • I can partition numbers by place value and add or subtract each part.
  • I can adjust a number to a friendly number and then compensate correctly.
  • I can shift both numbers in a subtraction by the same amount to keep the difference the same.
  • I can look at a calculation and justify which strategy is the most efficient.

The Big Idea

A good mathematician looks at the numbers before choosing a method.
The algorithm always works. It is almost never the fastest.

Today you are building a toolkit of three strategies, and learning to pick the right tool for the numbers in front of you.

Strategy 1: Partitioning

Split each number into its place value parts. Add the parts that match. Put the total back together.

364 + 253
1 300+60+4
2 200+50+3
3 Hundreds: 300 + 200 = 500
4 Tens: 60 + 50 = 110
5 Ones: 4 + 3 = 7
6 Recombine: 500 + 110 + 7 = 617
In your book

Copy the six steps above, one line each. Then do 526 + 341 the same way.

Learn this

Partitioning is the safe, everyday strategy. It works for every addition and every subtraction, no matter what the numbers look like.

Card 5 of 14

Strategy 2: Levelling

Adjust one number to a friendly number. Do the easy calculation. Then pay back what you borrowed.

268 + 97
1 Look at 97. It is only 3 away from 100.
2 Adjust: 268 + 100 = 368 added 3 too many
3 Compensate: 368 − 3 = 365 give the 3 back
4 So 268 + 97 = 365
The question to ask yourself

Did I add too much or too little? If you added too much, take it back off. If you added too little, add the rest on.

In your book

Copy steps 1 to 4. Circle the word compensate. Then do 435 + 96.

Learn this

Use levelling when one number sits just below or just above a friendly number like 100, 200 or 1000.

Card 6 of 14

Strategy 3: Constant Difference Subtraction only

Subtraction measures the gap between two numbers. Slide both numbers along by the same amount and the gap does not change.

704 − 296
1 296 is only 4 away from 300. That is the number we want.
Before: the gap we need to measure
Slide both numbers up 4. The gap is exactly the same size.
2 Now the easy one: 708 − 300 = 408. So 704 − 296 = 408.
In your book

Draw both number lines. Write 704 − 296 = 708 − 300 = 408. Then do 602 − 397.

Learn this

Works for subtraction only. Subtraction is a gap, and sliding both numbers together never changes the gap.

Card 7 of 14

The Trap: Levelling a Subtraction

Here is a student's work on 83 − 29. Something has gone wrong.

1 29 is 1 away from 30, so I will use 30.
2 83 − 30 = 53
3 I used 1 extra, so I take another 1 off. 53 − 1 = 52
The error

She took away 30 when she only needed to take away 29. She has taken 1 too much away already. Taking another 1 off makes it worse.

3 Give the extra 1 back: 53 + 1 = 54. So 83 − 29 = 54.
The rule that fixes it

Adding too much means you take the extra off.   268 + 100 becomes − 3

Taking away too much means you give the extra back.   83 − 30 becomes + 1

In your book

Write the heading The Compensation Trap. Copy both lines of the rule that fixes it.

Learn this

Always ask what you did to the calculation, then do the opposite to fix it. Never guess the direction.

Card 8 of 14

Which One, When?

Read the numbers first. They tell you which tool to reach for.

Partitioning

Split into hundreds, tens and ones. Add or subtract each part, then recombine.

Use when: no number is close to a friendly number. 364 + 253

Levelling

Round one number to a friendly number, calculate, then compensate.

Use when: one number ends in 8, 9, 98, 99. 268 + 97

Constant Difference

Slide both numbers by the same amount so the second one becomes friendly.

Use when: subtracting, and the second number is just under a hundred. 704 − 296
The one that catches people out

Constant difference is for subtraction only. If you slide both numbers in an addition, the total changes.

In your book

Rule three columns. Head them Partitioning, Levelling, Constant Difference. Write one Use when line in each.

Learn this

Choosing the strategy is the skill being assessed today, not just getting the answer.

Go Deeper: Constant Difference Investigations

These tasks push past finding answers. You will explore, make a claim, and then work out whether your claim survives.

Specialising
Write down any subtraction, for example 152 − 48. Work out the answer. Now add the same amount to both numbers and work out that new subtraction as well.
Do this with at least three different subtractions. What happens to the answer each time?
A mathematician's response

I started with 152 − 48, which is 104. Then I added 2 to both numbers and got 154 − 50, which is also 104. That surprised me, so I thought I had made an arithmetic slip.

I checked it with different numbers. 83 − 29 is 54. Adding 1 to both gives 84 − 30, which is 54 again.

Then I tried a bigger jump to see if the size of the jump mattered. 1000 − 678 is 322, and adding 22 to both gives 1022 − 700, which is 322. So the size of the jump does not seem to matter at all.

Three times out of three the answer stayed exactly the same. I am starting to think something is going on here that is not a coincidence.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
"If you add the same amount to both numbers in a subtraction, the answer never changes."
Is that always true, sometimes true, or never true? Try hard to find an example that breaks it before you decide.
A mathematician's response

My conjecture is always true, but I did not want to say that until I had tried to break it.

First I went hunting for a counterexample. I tried making the answer 0: 100 − 100 is 0, and adding 50 to both gives 150 − 150, still 0. No luck.

Then I tried making the answer go below zero, because I thought that might behave differently. 5 − 12 is -7, and 8 − 15 is also -7. Still no luck.

I even tried sliding the numbers the other way, taking 3 off both instead of adding. 7 − 3 is 4, and 4 − 0 is 4. It still held.

I could not break it, and I think I know why. A subtraction is measuring the gap between two numbers. When both numbers slide the same distance along the number line, the gap between them cannot change. So my conjecture is always true.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be proved.
Justifying
A student says: "Constant difference is brilliant, so I will use it on additions too. For 47 + 38 I will add 2 to both numbers to make 49 + 40, which is 89."
Is she right? If not, explain exactly what went wrong, and work out how much her answer is out by and why.
A mathematician's response

I worked out the real answer first. 47 + 38 is 85, and she got 89. So she is out by 4.

My first thought was that she had just made a mistake in her adding, but 49 + 40 really is 89. Her arithmetic is fine. The method is what is wrong.

Then I noticed where the 4 came from. She added 2 to one number and 2 to the other, so she put an extra 2 + 2 = 4 into the total. I tested that idea on another one: 268 + 97 is 365, and adding 3 to both gives 271 + 100 = 371, which is out by 6. That is 3 + 3. The rule holds.

This is the real reason. Subtraction measures a gap, so both numbers can move together and the gap survives. Addition measures a total, and anything you add to either number goes straight into the total.

There is a version that does work for addition, though. Add to one number and take the same amount off the other. 47 + 2 = 49 and 38 − 2 = 36, and 49 + 36 = 85. That is the correct answer, because what one number gained the other one lost.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just checking whether an answer is right, you are examining whether the reason given is correct. A right answer with a wrong reason is still a problem in mathematics.
