🔓 Go Deeper: Interpreter Investigations

These tasks will push your thinking beyond finding answers. You will explore, make conjectures, and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
Start with 1/2. Write it as a decimal and a percentage. Now halve it to get 1/4 and write that as a decimal and a percentage. Keep halving: 1/8, then 1/16, then 1/32.
What happens to the decimals and the percentages each time you halve? What do you notice?
A mathematician's response

I started with the ones I already knew. 1/2 is 0.5 and 50%. Halve it and I get 1/4, which is 0.25 and 25%. So far so good: the decimal halved and the percentage halved.

Then I tried to guess the next one before working it out. I thought 1/8 might be 0.12 because half of 25 is roughly 12. But that was not exact. Half of 25 is 12.5, not 12. So 1/8 is 0.125 and 12.5%. My guess was close but wrong, and being close is not the same as being right.

I kept going. 1/16 is 0.0625 and 6.25%. 1/32 is 0.03125 and 3.125%.

What I noticed: the denominator doubles every time while the decimal and the percentage halve every time. I also noticed the decimals get longer and longer. At 1/2 I needed one decimal place. At 1/32 I needed five. So I could keep halving forever and never run out of decimals.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
A traveller in the valley says: "Every fraction can be written as a percentage that is a whole number."
Is this always true, sometimes true, or never true? Test at least five different fractions and write your conjecture.
A mathematician's response

My first instinct was always true, because every fraction I could think of worked. 1/2 is 50%. 1/4 is 25%. 1/5 is 20%. 1/10 is 10%. 1/100 is 1%. Five out of five whole numbers.

But then I realised I had only picked fractions that divide neatly into 100. That felt like cheating, so I deliberately looked for one that did not. I tried 1/8. To find the percentage I worked out 100 divided by 8, which is 12.5. So 1/8 is 12.5%, and that is not a whole number.

Then I tried 1/3. 100 divided by 3 is 33.333... and the digits never stop. So 1/3 is about 33.3%, which is not a whole number either.

My conjecture: sometimes true. It works when the denominator divides exactly into 100, like 2, 4, 5, 10, 20, 25, 50 and 100. It does not work when the denominator does not, like 3, 8 or 6. One counterexample was enough to prove it is not always true.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be proved.
Justifying
A student in the valley writes: "1/3 must be 0.3, because there is a 3 in the fraction and a 3 in the decimal."
Is the student right? Use the hundred square, or a calculation, to explain why. What has gone wrong in their thinking?
A mathematician's response

My first thought was that the student might be right, because 0.3 does look close to a third. So I decided to test it rather than guess.

Here is the test I used. If 1/3 really were 0.3, then three thirds should make one whole. So 0.3 add 0.3 add 0.3 should equal 1. But it equals 0.9. There is 0.1 missing, so 0.3 cannot be a third.

I checked it on the hundred square too. 0.3 shades 30 squares. If I shade 30, then another 30, then another 30, I have shaded 90 squares and 10 are still empty. A third of 100 squares is 100 divided by 3, which is 33.333... squares. So 1/3 is 0.333..., with the 3s going on forever, and about 33.3%.

What went wrong in the student's thinking is that they matched the digits instead of the amount. The 3 in 1/3 tells you how many equal parts the whole is split into. The 3 in 0.3 tells you how many tenths you have. Those two 3s are doing completely different jobs. The same trap catches people with 1/4, which is 0.25 and not 0.4.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just checking whether an answer is right, you are examining whether the reason given is correct. A right answer with a wrong reason is still a problem in mathematics.
