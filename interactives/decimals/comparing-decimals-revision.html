Comparing Decimals

Which is bigger? Choose the correct symbol.

Learning Intention & Success Criteria ▼

Learning Intention

We are learning to compare decimal numbers using place value and record the comparison with the symbols <, > and =.

Success Criteria

  • I can read decimals up to thousandths and beyond.
  • I can compare two decimals by checking each place value column from left to right.
  • I can choose the correct symbol (<, > or =) to record a comparison.
  • I can explain why a decimal with more digits is not always the larger number.

Worked Example: 0.47 or 0.5? ▼

1

Line up the decimal points. Compare the tenths first: 0.47 has 4 tenths, and 0.5 has 5 tenths.

2

4 tenths is less than 5 tenths, so we can stop there. We do not even need to look at the hundredths.

3

Write the comparison with the symbol pointing at the smaller number: 0.47 < 0.5.

4

Watch out: 0.47 has more digits, but it is still smaller. The number of digits does not decide which decimal is bigger. Place value does.

🔓 Go Deeper: Comparing Decimals Investigations

These tasks will push your thinking beyond finding answers. You will explore, make conjectures, and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
Start with the decimal 0.4. Write three decimals with more decimal places that are all smaller than 0.4. Then write three decimals with more decimal places that are all larger than 0.4.
What do you notice about the tenths digit in each group?
A mathematician's response

At first I thought adding more digits would always make a number bigger, because 3999 is bigger than 4. So I tried 0.3999, expecting it to be bigger than 0.4. But that did not work: 0.3999 only has 3 tenths, so it is smaller than 0.4, which has 4 tenths.

That gave me the idea. For smaller decimals, I kept the tenths digit below 4: I chose 0.39, 0.152 and 0.3999. All three are smaller than 0.4 even though they have more decimal places.

For larger decimals, I either raised the tenths digit or kept it at 4 and added something after it: 0.41, 0.523 and 0.4001 are all larger than 0.4.

So I noticed the tenths digit did all the important work. The extra digits only mattered when the tenths digits were the same.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
"A decimal with more decimal places is larger than a decimal with fewer decimal places."
Is this always true, sometimes true, or never true? Write your conjecture and give examples that support it.
A mathematician's response

My conjecture: sometimes true.

I tested it with pairs of decimals. First I tried 0.35 and 0.3. The one with more places, 0.35, is larger. So far the claim looked true.

Then I tried 0.25 and 0.3. This time the one with more places, 0.25, is smaller, because 2 tenths is less than 3 tenths. So the claim is not always true.

I nearly stopped there, but then I found a third case: 0.50 and 0.5. The one with more places is not larger or smaller. It is equal, because a trailing zero adds nothing.

So the claim is only sometimes true. The number of decimal places tells you nothing on its own. You have to compare place by place, starting from the tenths.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be proved.
Justifying
A student says: "0.500001 must be larger than 0.72, because 500001 is a much bigger number than 72."
Is the student right? Explain exactly where their reasoning goes wrong, using place value.
A mathematician's response

I started by checking whether the student's answer could be right before worrying about their reasoning. I compared the two decimals place by place. In the tenths column, 0.500001 has 5 tenths and 0.72 has 7 tenths. Since 5 tenths is less than 7 tenths, 0.500001 is smaller. The student's answer is wrong.

Then I looked at where the reasoning broke down. The student read the digits after the decimal point as if they were a whole number: 500001 versus 72. I tried to see if that trick ever works, and I found it only works when both decimals have the same number of decimal places, like 0.72 and 0.59.

The digits after the point are not a whole number. Each digit lives in its own column, and the columns get 10 times smaller as you move right. The 5 in 0.500001 is worth 5 tenths, and almost all the other digits are zeros worth nothing. So 0.500001 is only a tiny bit more than one half, while 0.72 is nearly three quarters.

I concluded that 0.500001 < 0.72, and the mistake was treating the decimal part as a whole number instead of comparing place by place.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just checking whether an answer is right, you are examining whether the reason given is correct. A right answer with a wrong reason is still a problem in mathematics.
