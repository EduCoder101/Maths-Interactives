Learning Intention & Success Criteria ▼

Learning Intention

We are learning to convert decimals to fractions using place value.

Success Criteria

  • I can name the place value of the final digit in a decimal.
  • I can use the place value of the final digit as the denominator of my fraction.
  • I can write the decimal digits as the numerator of my fraction.
  • I can recognise common decimals such as 0.25 and 0.5 as simplified fractions.

Worked example: converting 0.29 ▼

1

Write 0.29 in a place value chart. The 2 sits in the tenths column and the 9 sits in the hundredths column.

2

Find the column of the final digit. The 9 is in the hundredths column, so we are counting hundredths.

3

The place value of the final digit becomes the denominator. Hundredths means the denominator is 100.

4

The decimal digits become the numerator. We have 29 hundredths, so 0.29 = 29/100.

🔓 Go Deeper: Decimal and Fraction Investigations

These tasks will push your thinking beyond finding answers. You'll explore, make conjectures, and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
Use the place value method to convert 0.5, 0.50 and 0.500 to fractions. Then simplify each fraction as far as you can.
What do you notice about the three fractions before simplifying? And after?
A mathematician's response

I started with 0.5. The final digit is in the tenths column, so I wrote 5/10. Then 0.50: the final digit is in the hundredths column, so I wrote 50/100. And 0.500 gave me 500/1000.

At first I thought I had made a mistake, because the three fractions looked completely different. Three different numerators, three different denominators. But then I simplified. 5/10 divides by 5 to give 1/2. 50/100 divides by 50 to give 1/2. And 500/1000 divides by 500 to give 1/2.

All three decimals are the same number in disguise. The place value method gives different-looking fractions, but simplifying reveals they were 1/2 all along.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
"Adding a zero to the end of a decimal changes its value."
Is this always true, sometimes true, or never true? Test it with at least three decimals of your own, then write your conjecture with the evidence that supports it.
A mathematician's response

My first instinct was "of course it changes the value, the number looks longer". So I tested it. I took 0.2 and added a zero to get 0.20. As fractions: 2/10 and 20/100. Are they equal? If I multiply the top and bottom of 2/10 by 10, I get exactly 20/100. Equal.

I tried 0.35 and 0.350: that is 35/100 and 350/1000, and multiplying top and bottom by 10 again shows they match. Then 1.4 and 1.40: the same thing happened.

My conjecture: never true. Adding a zero to the end multiplies the numerator by 10 and the denominator by 10 at the same time, and doing the same thing to the top and bottom of a fraction never changes its value. But I noticed something important on the way: it is a different story if the zero goes in the middle. 0.25 and 0.205 are definitely not equal, because the digits have moved to new columns.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be proved.
Justifying
A student converts two decimals and says: "0.25 = 25/100 and 0.4 = 4/10. Since 25 is bigger than 4, that proves 0.25 is bigger than 0.4."
Both conversions are correct, but the conclusion is wrong. Where exactly does the reasoning break, and how could the student fix it?
A mathematician's response

I checked the two conversions first, and the student is right about both: 0.25 really is 25/100 and 0.4 really is 4/10. So for a moment the argument looked convincing, because 25 is certainly bigger than 4.

Then I spotted the problem: the student compared the numerators while ignoring the denominators. Hundredths and tenths are different-sized pieces. Having 25 tiny pieces is not automatically more than 4 big pieces. It is like saying 25 cents is more money than 4 dollars.

The fix is to make the pieces the same size before comparing. 4/10 is the same as 40/100. Now both fractions count hundredths, and 40 hundredths beats 25 hundredths. So 0.4 is bigger than 0.25, which the place value chart confirms: 4 tenths beats 2 tenths before we even look at the hundredths.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just checking whether an answer is right, you are examining whether the reason given is correct. A right answer with a wrong reason is still a problem in mathematics.
