Learning Intention & Success Criteria ▼

Learning Intention

We are learning to use addition, subtraction, multiplication and percentages with money to plan a budget, and to check our own calculations.

Success Criteria

  • I can find the total cost of a list of items and work out how much money is left.
  • I can calculate a daily running cost, including changing a weekly or yearly amount into a daily amount.
  • I can calculate income from the number of visitors and the price each one pays, and find the profit by subtracting the costs.
  • I can find a percentage of an amount, such as 10% or 40%, and explain what it means.

Go Deeper: Theme Park Investigations

These tasks push your thinking beyond finding answers. You will explore, make conjectures and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
A Category 1 ride costs $50 000 to build and $90 a day to run. A Category 2 ride costs $25 000 to build and $60 a day to run. Work out the total cost of owning each ride (building it plus running it) after 30 days. Then try 60 days and 90 days.
What happens to the gap between the two rides as the days go on? Does it stay the same?
A mathematician's response

I started with 30 days. Category 1: 30 x 90 = 2 700 to run, so 50 000 + 2 700 = 52 700. Category 2: 30 x 60 = 1 800, so 25 000 + 1 800 = 26 800. The gap is 25 900.

At first I guessed the gap would stay at 25 000, because that is the difference in building cost. It did not. So I tried 60 days: 55 400 and 28 600, gap 26 800. Then 90 days: 58 100 and 30 400, gap 27 700.

The gap grows by 900 every 30 days. That makes sense: the Category 1 ride costs 30 more a day to run, and 30 x 30 = 900. The big ride gets more expensive compared with the small one the longer you own it.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
"Adding a first aid centre is always worth it. It only costs $8 000 and it can lift your park a whole price category, which is $5 more from every visitor."
Is this always true, sometimes true, or never true? Write your conjecture and give examples from your own park to support it.
A mathematician's response

My conjecture: sometimes true.

I first thought it was always true, because $5 extra from every visitor sounds huge. I checked with a park that gets 50 visitors a day. That is 50 x 5 = 250 extra a day. The centre also costs $40 a day to run, so the real gain is 210 a day. 8 000 divided by 210 is about 38, so after 38 days it has paid for itself. Worth it.

Then I re-read the rubric. To reach Price Category 2 you also need two Category 1 rides, seven rides in total and four Category 3 facilities. If a park has only one Category 1 ride, the first aid centre lifts nothing. The owner has spent 8 000 and pays 40 a day for no extra income.

So the claim is only true when the first aid centre is the last thing missing from a price category. That is why it is sometimes, not always.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be tested.
Justifying
A student says: "My profit went up by 10% each month for five months. So after five months my monthly profit is 50% bigger than it was in Month 1."
Is she right? Test it with a Month 1 profit of $1 000. Explain carefully what is going on.
A mathematician's response

It sounds right: five lots of 10% should be 50%. I tested it with 1 000.

Month 2: 1 000 + 100 = 1 100. Month 3: 10% of 1 100 is 110, so 1 210. Month 4: 1 331. Month 5: 1 464.10. Month 6: 1 610.51.

After five increases the profit is 1 610.51, which is about 61% bigger, not 50%. The student is wrong, and I can explain why. Each 10% is taken from the new month, which is bigger than the one before, so each increase is a bit larger than the last: 100, then 110, then 121, and so on. The increases themselves are growing. If every increase had been 100, she would have been right, but that is not what "10% each month" means.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just checking whether an answer is right, you are explaining why the reasoning works or fails.
