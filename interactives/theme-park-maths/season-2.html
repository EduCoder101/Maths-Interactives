Learning Intention & Success Criteria ▼

Learning Intention

We are learning to use percentages and multi-step calculations with large amounts of money to make and check business decisions.

Success Criteria

  • I can find 40%, one third, 10%, 20%, 30% and 11% of a large amount of money.
  • I can work out a running cost for a day and then for a whole season of 153 days.
  • I can calculate income from thousands of visitors and find a profit by subtracting the costs.
  • I can follow a chain of calculations (profit, tax, insurance, loan) in the right order and explain why the order matters.

Go Deeper: Season 2 Investigations

These tasks push your thinking beyond finding answers. You will explore, make conjectures and reason like a mathematician.

Specialising
Leaflets cost $50 000 and bring 10% more visitors. Without advertising, 29 510 people come in Season 2. Using your own park's entry fee and your own cafes, shops and stands, work out how much money the extra visitors would bring in. Then try radio ($100 000, 20%) and television ($250 000, 30%).
Does each kind of advertising pay for itself? Which one makes the most money after its cost?
A mathematician's response

I used a park with a $15 entry fee, 2 cafes, 1 shop and 1 ice cream stand. First I worked out what one visitor is worth: 15 at the gate, 24 at the two cafes, 4 at the shop and 3 on ice cream. That is $46 per visitor.

Leaflets: 10% of 29 510 is 2 951 extra visitors. 2 951 x 46 = 135 746. Take away the 50 000 and leaflets make 85 746.

At first I thought the gate alone would decide it: 2 951 x 15 is only 44 265, which is less than 50 000, so I nearly said leaflets lose money. Then I remembered the visitors spend inside the park too. That changed the answer completely.

Radio: 5 902 extra visitors x 46 = 271 492, minus 100 000, makes 171 492. Television: 8 853 x 46 = 407 238, minus 250 000, makes 157 238. For this park, radio wins, and television is not worth the extra 150 000. A park with more cafes would get a different answer.

Mathematician's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying specific examples to gather evidence, looking for a pattern before you make any claims.
Conjecturing
"A restaurant is always worth buying in Season 2. It costs $25 000, and half of all your visitors spend $24 there."
Is this always true, sometimes true, or never true? Think about costs, space and the one-third rule. Use numbers from your own park.
A mathematician's response

My conjecture: sometimes true, though it is true for every park in our class this season.

I started with the money. Half of 29 510 visitors is 14 755, and 14 755 x 24 = 354 120. The restaurant costs 25 000 to build and 100 a day to run, which is 15 300 for 153 days. Total cost 40 300. That is a profit of over 300 000. On money alone, it looks like always.

Then I asked when it would stop being true. Each visitor who eats brings in 12 on average (half of 24). To cover 40 300 you need 40 300 divided by 12, about 3 359 visitors. Season 2 has nearly ten times that, so money is not the problem.

The catch is elsewhere. A restaurant needs 6 empty squares. If a park is full, you must clear something, and clearing a Category 2 ride costs 60 000. And the 25 000 must fit inside your one third. A group with a small bank and a full park could not afford it. So: sometimes, because the rule depends on space and budget, not just on income.

Mathematician's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim that you believe is true, based on your evidence, but you know it still needs to be tested.
Justifying
A student says: "It does not matter whether you take the insurance off before or after the tax. You end up with the same amount." Test it with a profit of $1 000 000 and insurance of $150 000.
Is she right? Explain exactly why or why not, and say how big the difference is.
A mathematician's response

The booklet's way: tax first. 30% of 1 000 000 is 300 000, leaving 700 000. Then take off insurance: 700 000 - 150 000 = 550 000.

The student's way: insurance first. 1 000 000 - 150 000 = 850 000. Then 30% tax on that is 255 000, leaving 595 000.

They are not the same. The difference is 45 000. I wanted to know where that number came from, so I looked at the tax. In the first way the tax was 300 000; in the second it was 255 000. The gap is 45 000, which is exactly 30% of the 150 000 insurance.

So the student is wrong, and here is why: when you take insurance off first, the tax is charged on a smaller number, so you pay 30% less tax on the insurance amount. Subtracting and finding a percentage are not interchangeable. Order matters whenever a percentage is involved. The booklet says tax first, so that is the rule we follow, but it is worth knowing the business would rather it were the other way round.

Mathematician's thinking: You are justifying. That means you are not just checking whether an answer is right, you are explaining why the reasoning works or fails.
