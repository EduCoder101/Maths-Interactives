Lesson 1 · Length × Width, Both Ways

Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to calculate the area of a rectangle by multiplying, and to find a missing side by dividing.

Success Criteria

  • I can draw the area model to multiply a two-digit length by a one-digit width.
  • I can calculate an area and record it with the right square unit.
  • I can find a missing side when I know the area and the other side.
  • I can check an area answer with an estimate.
Today

Mini-whiteboards for the Hook, I Do and We Do. Then the Area Audit sheet, then your book under the heading 'Area: Both Ways'.

Card
Lesson 1 · Hook

My Radar Is Going Off

An advertisement in the paper reads:

Rug for sale. 3 m by 2 m. Area 5 m².

My Radar is going off. Why?

The error

The seller added the sides: 3 + 2 = 5. Area is not a length. Area is how many squares cover the rug.

3 × 2 = 6 m²
Watch for

Writing cm instead of cm², and adding instead of multiplying. Both come from thinking of area as a length. Ask "how many squares?" every time.

Lesson 1 · Hook

The Pivot

In Term 2 we learnt that area is length × width.

This week we use it both ways: forwards to find an area, and backwards to find a side.

Link to Division Week

Last week every division had a dividend, a divisor and a quotient. Today the area is the dividend, the known side is the divisor, and the missing side is the quotient.

Lesson 1 · I Do

Example 3: a square 15 cm on each side

Is its area 60 cm?

Perimeter, not area

15 + 15 + 15 + 15 = 60 cm is the distance around the edge. We want the squares inside.

15 rows of 15 squares.

110 × 15 = 150
25 × 15 = 75
150 + 75 = 225 cm²
Key idea

Area = length × width. A missing side = area ÷ the side you know. The unit for area is always a square unit.

Lesson 1 · Plenary

When is area a multiplication, and when is it a division?

Tell your partner. Then one pair tells the class.

Answer

Multiply when you know both sides and want the area. Divide when you know the area and one side and want the other side.

Lesson 2 · Same Fence, Different Field

Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning that rectangles with the same perimeter can have very different areas, and to find the one with the largest area.

Success Criteria

  • I can tell perimeter and area apart and use the right unit for each.
  • I can list every whole-number rectangle with a given perimeter.
  • I can find which one has the largest area and explain the pattern.
  • I can write an 'I noticed...' sentence with evidence from my table.
Today

String loop and grid paper for the investigation. Then your book under the heading 'Same Fence, Different Field'.

Card
Lesson 2 · Hook

Two farmers, 24 m of fence each

Farmer A builds a pen 11 m × 1 m. Farmer B builds 6 m × 6 m. Same fence. Which sheep have more room? How much more?

1A: 11 + 1 + 11 + 1 = 24 m
2B: 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24 m

Same perimeter.

1A: 11 × 1 = 11 m²
1A: 11 × 1 = 11 m²
2B: 6 × 6 = 36 m²

B has more than three times the room.

The Pivot

Same perimeter does not mean same area. Today we find out which rectangle wins, and why.

Watch for

"Bigger perimeter means bigger area." Keep the two columns side by side in every table so students see them move independently.

Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to find the area of a composite figure in more than one way, and to work out a side that is not labelled.

Success Criteria

  • I can split an L-shape into two rectangles and add their areas.
  • I can surround an L-shape with a rectangle and subtract the missing corner.
  • I can work out a missing side from the sides I am given.
  • I can check my answer by doing it a second way.

Card
Lesson 3 · Hook

No blocks this time

In Term 2 you found the area of L-shapes by counting blocks. Nobody is giving us blocks this time, and two sides are not even labelled. Can we still find the area?

Discuss

Most students say we need the missing sides first. Agree, and say we can work them out from the sides we have.

Watch for

Students who add all the labelled sides (a perimeter habit), and students who count a corner twice when they partition. Shade each rectangle a different colour so nothing overlaps.

Lesson 3 · I Do

Three ways to the same answer

The Hook shape: 10 m across the bottom, 8 m up the left, cut-out 4 m across and 3 m down.

1Top-left edge: 10 − 4 = 6 m
2Right-hand edge: 8 − 3 = 5 m
1Bottom rectangle 10 × 5 = 50 m²
2Top rectangle 6 × 3 = 18 m²
350 + 18 = 68 m²
1Left rectangle 6 × 8 = 48 m²
2Right rectangle 4 × 5 = 20 m²
348 + 20 = 68 m²
1Whole rectangle 10 × 8 = 80 m²
2Missing corner 4 × 3 = 12 m²
380 − 12 = 68 m²
Key idea

Three ways, one answer. If two ways disagree, one of them has a mistake. Always do it a second way to check.

Two answers that disagree

Two students found the We Do shape. One says 48 m². The other says 60 m². Which is wrong, and how do you know without redoing it?

Answer

60 m² is the whole 9 × 7 rectangle (63) with almost nothing taken away, so it is too big. The cut-out is 15 m², so the answer must be less than 63 by 15.

Lesson 4 · Hectares: Compare and Convert

Learning Intention and Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to convert between square metres, hectares and square kilometres so that we can compare large areas.

Success Criteria

  • I can explain 1 ha = 10,000 m² and 1 km² = 100 ha with a drawing.
  • I can convert an area in square metres to hectares by dividing by 10,000.
  • I can find the area of a large rectangle and record it in hectares.
  • I can put areas in different units in order.
Today

Mini-whiteboards. Then your book under the heading 'Hectares', with the three conversion rules in a box at the top.

Card
Lesson 4 · Hook

Three paddocks for sale

Paddock A is 2 ha. Paddock B is 15,000 m². Paddock C is 100 m × 100 m.

Which is biggest? Which is smallest? Be ready to prove it.

1A: 2 ha = 20,000 m²
1A: 2 ha = 20,000 m²
2C: 100 × 100 = 10,000 m² = 1 ha
C, B, A

You cannot compare until the units match.

The Pivot

In Term 2 you met the hectare and estimated areas on Google Earth. Today we convert, so we can compare any two areas.

Watch for

"1 hectare is 100 m²" (confusing the side length with the area) and dividing by 1,000 instead of 10,000. Draw the 100 m square every time and count its 10 × 10 blocks of 10 m.
